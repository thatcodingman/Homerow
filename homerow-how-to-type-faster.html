How to type faster

A practical guide to raising your WPM

Plenty of people stall right around the average typing speed (35-45 WPM) because they type the way they always have: a few fingers, a lot of glancing at the keys. The good news is that speed is mostly habit, and habits can be rebuilt with a few weeks of short, deliberate practice. Here is what actually moves the needle. Not sure where you stand yet? Take the typing test first to get a baseline.

1. Learn the home row

Rest your fingers on A S D F (left hand) and J K L ; (right hand), with your index fingers on F and J. Those two keys usually have small raised bumps so you can find them without looking. Every reach starts from the home row and returns to it, which is what keeps your hands oriented without any glancing down.

2. Put accuracy before speed

Every mistake costs a backspace and breaks your rhythm, so a slightly slower, clean pace almost always beats a fast, sloppy one. Watch the gap between your WPM and your raw WPM on the results screen: a big gap means errors are costing you more than you think. Get accuracy high first and speed follows.

3. Stop looking at the keys

Every glance down breaks your flow and makes you lose your place in the text. Keep your eyes on the words, not the keyboard. It feels slower at first, and your numbers may even dip for a week or two, but this is the single biggest habit change for most people.

4. Give each finger its own reach

Each finger owns a column of keys. Use the finger that belongs to the key rather than stretching a nearby one, and snap back to the home row after each reach. Hunting and pecking with only the index fingers is the most common ceiling on typing speed.

5. Aim for a steady rhythm

Fast typists are smooth, not jerky. Bursts of speed followed by stalls are slower than an even, metronome-like pace, and uneven pacing is exactly what the consistency number and the pace graph on your results screen reveal. Try to keep the same tempo from the first word to the last.

6. Practice little and often

Short daily sessions beat one long weekly one, because muscle memory builds through repetition spread over days. A handful of 30- or 60-second tests, ten to fifteen minutes in total, is plenty. Stop when your accuracy starts slipping from fatigue.

7. Drill your weak keys

After each test, the "Where you slipped" heatmap shades the keys you missed most. Note the darkest ones and spend a few minutes typing slowly through words that use them. Fixing your three or four worst keys usually lifts your score more than general practice.

8. Graduate to real text

Words mode is great for measuring raw speed, but real writing has capital letters and punctuation. Once Words mode feels comfortable, switch to Punctuation and Quotes mode to practice the Shift key and pinky reaches you actually use every day. Expect your WPM to drop a little there; that is normal.

9. Set up your posture and keyboard

Sit with your elbows around 90 degrees, wrists in a neutral position (not bent up or resting hard on the desk), and the screen at eye level. Tense shoulders slow you down and cause strain, so take short breaks, especially during longer practice sessions.

How fast should you aim to be?

  • Under 30 WPM — beginner, still building muscle memory for key positions
  • 35–45 WPM — average for most adults doing everyday typing
  • 50–65 WPM — solid, comfortable touch-typing speed
  • 65–90 WPM — fast, well above the office-work average
  • 100+ WPM — professional transcriptionist or competitive-typist territory
