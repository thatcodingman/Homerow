Typing posture and ergonomics

A comfortable setup helps speed and endurance

Good posture will not magically raise your WPM, but a poor setup makes you tense, tired, and error-prone, especially in longer tests. These are common, widely recommended habits for typing comfortably. If you have pain or numbness, this page is not medical advice; see a doctor or physical therapist.

Chair and body position

  • Sit back with your lower back supported and feet flat on the floor
  • Keep your knees roughly level with your hips, around a right angle
  • Let your shoulders drop and relax instead of hunching toward your ears

Keyboard and arm position

  • Place the keyboard so your elbows rest near your sides at about a right angle
  • Keep your wrists in a neutral, fairly straight line with your forearms rather than bent up or down
  • Hover your hands over the keys instead of resting your wrists on the desk while typing
  • Tap keys with a light touch; hammering them wastes energy and tires your fingers

Screen position

Put the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away. Looking down at a laptop for long stretches strains the neck, so consider a stand and an external keyboard if you type a lot.

Breaks and variety

Pause every 20 to 30 minutes to stand, stretch, and look at something far away. Short, frequent breaks are easier to keep up than rare long ones. In typing practice, stop when your hands feel tight or your error count climbs.

Does posture affect speed?

Indirectly, yes. A relaxed setup keeps your fingers on the home row with less effort, which supports accuracy, and accuracy drives speed. If your scores drop late in a long test, check the consistency number and your tension level; our accuracy guide covers fatigue in more detail.

When to get help

Ongoing pain, tingling, or numbness in your hands, wrists, or arms is worth taking seriously. Stop practicing and talk to a healthcare professional rather than trying to push through it.
